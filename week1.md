# Week 1 — Cane Ridge (August 1801)

[← All weeks](.)

**Central theme:** A wild experience of unity, which changed the participants — and
was left standing as a profound symbol of how the church needed to come together
across denominational and authoritative lines, and evidence that the Spirit was
calling them forward to this.

---

## Opening narration (working draft)

*One staging detail is still being reconciled against the sources: this draft has
the communion at outdoor cross-shaped tables; the documented account has long tables
inside the meetinghouse, served over eight-plus sittings. The outline below reflects
the documented version.*

In August of 1801, 20-30,000 people converged on the tiny town of Cane Ridge,
Kentucky. Wagons clogged the road into town, people backed up for miles. All of
them heading for what became one of America's first revival camp meetings, the
launch of a chain of events known as the Second Great Awakening. At this revival,
people of various denominations and traditions, including Presbyterians, Methodists,
Baptists, and folks both black and white, came together to listen to vigorous
proclamation of the gospel. The response was electric, many people breaking out in
physical agitations, shaking, running, contorting, falling down as if dead, until
they rose again proclaiming their allegiance to God and Christ. Many people,
including the preachers, didn't know what to make of these effects, but they seemed
to be producing genuine repentance and transformation, changing the course of
people's lives from then on. And something else was being produced: the retreat of
the "party spirit", and the rise of a sense of unity in Christ. The preachers from
each of these denominations, who were formally at odds, who were forbidden by their
own church hierarchies and authorities from participating in communion with each
other, were suddenly not only preaching together, but at the pinnacle of this event,
came together to share communion with everyone. Long tables were arranged outdoors
in the shape of the cross, and all people gathered around, distributing large
servings of bread and wine to each person.

This was the birth of what we know as the Churches of Christ.

This event became a movement for unity, simplicity, and freedom in Christ. A
movement that opposed all the authorities who divided the body of Christ, all the
obstacles to shared communion, all the barriers to simple proclamation. It began,
not with a programmatic initiative, but with the sense that the Spirit was doing
something, and calling people into the recognition of our already-existing freedom
and unity in Christ. It began, too, with the sense that the kingdom of God was
breaking into the world, and that the job of Christians was to strive to live that
out, "on earth as it is in heaven".

At the epicenter of this event, and eventually this movement, diligently observing
and documenting everything that he could, was a young Presbyterian minister named
Barton W. Stone. He was the preacher for the Cane Ridge church, newly ordained,
newly married, but already an experienced traveler through America's frontier. And
what he was seeing would not allow him to toe the party line. He could not exclude
those he was being told to exclude, he could not refuse those he was being told to
refuse, he could not preach a convoluted message of Westminster-approved
metaphysics, when Christ Jesus' simple invitation to "come" was changing lives, and
bringing people to repentance and transformation. Before this moment, he wrote,
religious practice had all but died in this region of country. After this moment,
even the secular rationalists and deists were falling down at Jesus' feet. This
compelled him to be willing to challenge everything he knew, and to follow the
evidence wherever it would lead.

That's going to lead us to our next session, where we see just how much he, and
others with him, were willing to sacrifice for the sake of Jesus' simple call to
freedom and unity.

But I want to draw out a couple of points right here at the beginning.

First, the defining ideas of this movement--ideas like unity and freedom; free
investigation of truth and a resistance to every authority who would attempt to
divide the body of Christ; the significance of eschatology; the simplicity of
communion and baptism and scripture and singing; decentralized authority and the
priesthood of all believers--all of these ideas are connected and arise from the
experience of the young American church on the frontier, watching and witnessing
the work of the Spirit.

Second, that if you come from another tradition, whether Presbyterian or Methodist
or Baptist or Anglican or Catholic or whatever it may be, then in a sense, we do
too. In a very real way, our tradition arose when people of many other traditions
came together, following scripture and the Spirit, in pursuit of a greater
realization of unity. And as they came together, they brought the riches of their
traditions with them, and sought to hold onto whatever was good from wherever it
came. So wherever you come from--if you grew up Church of Christ, or came to it
later, if you married into it, or if you are Catholic or Baptist or Methodist or
anything else--then in a real way, all of us together are doing what our spiritual
predecessors were doing, coming together for a deeper sense of unity and freedom in
Christ, bringing the riches of our traditions together and placing them at Jesus'
feet. That is the tradition we'll be exploring in this class, and the tradition
behind Otter Creek itself. I invite you to continue the exploration of this story
with us.

## Beat-by-beat outline (second draft of the telling)

*A restructured telling of the same story, built around one reveal. Key sources:
Barton Stone's autobiography (Stone & Rogers, 1847), Richard McNemar's* The Kentucky
Revival *(1807), and the Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement.*

### Movement I — The Roads (cold open)

**1. The roads are clogged.** August 1801, Bourbon County, Kentucky. Open inside
the scene, no preamble: wagons, carriages, horsemen, footmen, backed up for miles.
Stone's own words: *"The roads were literally crowded with wagons, carriages,
horsemen, and footmen, moving to the solemn camp. The sight was affecting."*
McNemar counted 135 wheel-carriages encamped; one participant: *"The noise was like
the roar of Niagara."*

**2. Converging on… what?** Hold the question — don't answer it yet. Size it
instead: military men on the ground guessed twenty to thirty thousand; even the
cautious modern figure of ten thousand at once is a tenth of Kentucky, in a state
whose largest towns were villages. The biggest gathering anyone in America had ever
seen — and the audience doesn't know yet what it's *for*.

### Movement II — The Week (the wildness)

**3. The encampment.** Four or five preachers speaking at once from stumps, wagons,
and stands, *"without confusion."* Presbyterians, Methodists, Baptists — *"all
appeared cordially united in it — of one mind and one soul… We all engaged in
singing the same songs of praise — all united in prayer — all preached the same
things — free salvation urged upon all."*

**4. The falling** — stage it plainly and slowly, since no one today expects it.
People drop *"as men slain in battle,"* lie breathless and motionless for hours,
revive with a groan or a shriek or a fervent prayer, then rise proclaiming
deliverance. The fallen are dragged clear of the crowd so small circles can pray and
sing over them. An old minister named James Crawford tries to keep count and gives
up somewhere around three thousand. Land the disorientation honestly: nobody there
fully understood it either — including the preachers.

**5. The reveal.** All of this — the roads, the roar, the falling — was gathered
around **a table**. This wasn't a "revival" (that label came later) and it wasn't
the first camp meeting. It was a scheduled *communion service*: a four-day Scottish
sacramental occasion — preparation Friday and Saturday, the Lord's Supper on Sunday,
thanksgiving Monday. Twenty thousand people clogged the roads of Kentucky to get to
the Lord's table. (This reframes everything the audience just pictured, and it's the
thesis of the semester.)

- *Optional grace note here (or at beat 11):* Stone's own line — *"Long tables were
  spread with provisions, and all invited to eat."* These are the encampment's food
  tables, not the communion tables — but the image of "all invited to eat" is
  Stone's own, and it is the sermon in miniature.

### Movement III — Sunday (the table, and the crack in it)

**6. The tables in the rain.** Sunday it poured. Robert Marshall preaches the
traditional "action sermon" fencing the table; then, inside a log meetinghouse built
for four hundred, long tables are set up in the aisles, and communicants file in
about a hundred at a sitting — eight or more sittings, on into the late afternoon.
Eight hundred to eleven hundred communicants, including perhaps two hundred
Methodists at a Presbyterian table.

**7. The crack — the Burke incident.** It almost didn't happen. Some Presbyterians
balked at communing with Methodists, and under their pressure Stone asked William
Burke to publicly state Methodist doctrine before the sacrament could be arranged —
a doctrinal toll-booth at the door of the table. Burke took the insult and
challenged Stone to make the Presbyterians do the same. Stone withdrew the request.
Then Burke climbed a fallen tree a hundred feet east of the meetinghouse and
preached anyway, another Methodist holding an umbrella lashed to a pole over his
head, and the crowd fell around him. The united sacrament almost cracked on Sunday
morning — and the recovery is part of the miracle.

**[8. The honest edges.]** *(Placement still being decided.)* The table was
administered only by Presbyterian ministers. The Baptists mostly wouldn't commune —
their association was meeting near Lexington that same week. A Black congregation
was preached to separately at the camp's edge, in a slave state. The unity was an
achievement under strain, not a given — which is exactly why it marked people for
life.

### Movement IV — The Witness

**9. Barton W. Stone.** Twenty-eight years old, pastor of Cane Ridge, five weeks
married, the man who spent the summer promoting this as a "united sacrament" — at
the epicenter, watching everything. His posture toward the wildness is the model: he
grants *"much fanaticism,"* debunks the barking-exercise legend from the inside —
and yet: *"I am always hurt to hear people speak lightly of this work. I always
think they speak of what they know nothing about."* He never got over that week. He
spent the next forty years trying to build a church that felt like it.

### Movement V — Lessons

**10. If you come from another tradition — in a sense, we do too.** Our tradition
begins with people of different Christian streams coming together across
denominational lines, bringing the riches of their traditions with them.

**11. The central practice of that unity was a shared table** — opened beyond the
limits authority had placed on it. Two forward hooks: the demand Stone withdrew from
Burke is the very demand a presbytery will enforce against Thomas Campbell six years
later (next week's story); and the landing at home — our weekly open communion is
Cane Ridge carried forward, the frontier field become a Table set every Sunday.

**12. Wildness and rationality.** Stone's third way — neither credulity nor
mockery — opens a thread the class will return to.

**13. Bigger than us.** Participants carried the work to Ohio and as far as North
Carolina; the camp meeting became the signature institution of Methodist expansion;
McNemar himself became a Shaker. The wildness escaped in every direction, for good
and ill — Cane Ridge belongs to American Christianity, not just to us.

**14. Close.** Return to the opening question: what were they converging on?
Something that looked, for a week, like the age to come. Next session: what it cost
the people who couldn't forget it.
