# Week 2 — Thomas Campbell's Break (1807–1809)

[← All weeks](.)

**Central theme:** The break did not begin as a doctrine; it began as hospitality.
When the institution's fences and the gospel's welcome conflict, the fences are the
thing without divine warrant — and the man who opened the table genuinely did not
want to leave.

**Companion week to Week 1:** both weeks turn on a communion table someone tried to
fence. At Cane Ridge the gatekeepers backed down and the table opened; here the
fence fights back.

---

## Opening narration (working draft)

*A few details in this draft are still being corrected against the sources: the
intruders in the Ireland church scene were government cavalry hunting suspected
rebels (not a criminal gang); the Burgher oath concerned a few Scottish towns, not
counties; the complaint went to the local presbytery (the synod was the appeal
court); and the ending will reflect that Campbell ultimately withdrew — he was never
formally expelled.*

In 1807 in the rural regions of Appalachia, 70 miles outside of Pittsburgh,
immigrant families are scattered among the nooks and crannies of the Appalachian
frontier. Many of them are faithful Presbyterians, who have not received communion
in years. Not because of the lack of a minister, but because of the lack of a
minister from their specific sub-sect of the Presbyterians. According to the rules
of these Presbyterian sects, no one could give or receive communion from a member of
a different Presbyterian sub-sect. And so, many of these faithful Christians had
gone years without being invited to the communion table.

Into this remote region is sent Thomas Campbell, a newly arrived immigrant himself;
a respected Presbyterian minister from Ireland.

He was from the Associate Synod of North America, of the Anti-Burgher Seceder
Presbyterians…each of those terms designating a division from another group of
Christians.

In Ireland, he had been immersed in an incredibly divided and violent culture—where
the tensions between Catholics and Protestants had led to violent gangs roaming the
countryside, killing and plundering. At one point, Thomas Campbell was leading a
Sunday church service when a gang broke in, threatening violence; he immediately
began praying from the Psalms, and the gang leader stopped in his tracks, bowed his
head, and then turned around and left.

In Ireland, Thomas Campbell had fought to bridge the sectarian divides, trying to
broker a unity between the Burgher and the anti-Burgher Presbyterians, seeing as
their point of dispute was not even about something in Ireland, but about
disagreement on a political matter involving three counties in Scotland, a whole
country away. This effort had gained a lot of traction, before ultimately being shut
down by the higher levels of the Presbyterian church hierarchy.

Now in America, Campbell found a country mercifully free of sectarian violence, and
seemingly open to new bridges of unity. And so as he ventured into the Appalachian
hills to minister to scattered immigrant families, he was moved to offer communion
not just to those of his own branch of Presbyterians, but to other Presbyterians who
had been rejected from the table.

In these gatherings, he preached on the need to set sectarianism aside, and to come
together in Christian unity with other Christian brothers and sisters.

This was well-received, and Campbell was heartened about the possibilities for the
growth of Christian brotherhood. But back in Pittsburgh, things did not go so well.
One of Campbell's travel companions had privately looked on at this process with
disapproval, and once they had returned, he reported to the synod, and Thomas
Campbell was brought up on a heresy trial.

The charges brought against him included the claim that he had allegedly:

- Said it was okay to occasionally listen to a sermon from a Presbyterian of a
  different sect
- Said it was okay for elders to pray and worship with a congregation when a
  minister was absent

But the greatest charge of all was that Thomas Campbell refused to let the doctrines
of a particular Presbyterian sect determine who could and could not receive
Communion.

And so Campbell was brought to trial, and judged guilty.

Campbell appealed, and in 1808, Campbell stood before the assembled Associate Synod
and read:

> "Honored Brethren: Before you come to a final issue… let me entreat you to pause a
> moment…"

> "To refuse any one his just privilege, is it not to oppress and injure? In
> proportion to the magnitude and importance of the privilege withheld, is not the
> injustice done in withholding it to be estimated? If so, how great the injustice,
> how highly aggravated the injury will appear, to thrust out from communion a
> Christian brother…for saying and doing none other things than those which our
> Divine Lord and his holy apostles have taught and enjoined to be spoken and
> done…"?

> "It is…because I have no confidence, either in my own infallibility or in that of
> others, that I absolutely refuse…to acknowledge as obligatory…or to impose upon
> others, anything as of Divine obligation for which I cannot produce a 'Thus saith
> the Lord'"

If Christ had not imposed it as a test of fellowship between Christian brothers,
then Campbell believed he had no right to impose such a division himself.

Campbell went on to assure his judges that just as he could not submit in good
conscience to the divisions they wanted to impose, he would not in turn impose his
own conscience upon them. Instead, he called all to continue to engage in the work
of the gospel according to their best understanding, as there was much work to be
done.

Campbell had hoped his statement would lead to reconciliation, and a new birth of
unity. It did not. And so Campbell was disassociated from the Association, and they
stripped from him everything they had the authority to remove.

And then Campbell discovered that this meant very little.

He began preaching under a maple tree in a grove, and Presbyterians sick and tired
of sectarianism flocked to him. Soon this grew into an association, and then a
church, and then a movement. And this movement took as one of its rallying cries a
line from Thomas Campbell's Declaration and Address, written shortly after:

"The Church of Christ upon earth is essentially, intentionally, and constitutionally
one."

We'll return to that next week.

## Beat-by-beat outline

*Key sources: Richardson's* Memoirs of Alexander Campbell *vol. 1 (1868 — the
family's account, with the full text of Campbell's appeal); Hanna's* Thomas
Campbell: Seceder and Christian Union Advocate *(1935 — transcribes the Chartiers
Presbytery minutes, including the seven charges and Campbell's answers verbatim);
Foster's* A Life of Alexander Campbell *(2020).*

### Movement I — The Fences (cold open)

**1. The settlers without a Supper.** Western Pennsylvania, 1807. Thin settlements
above Pittsburgh — in Richardson's words, "various fragments of religious parties,
which, having floated off from the Old World upon the tide of emigration, had been
thrown together in the circling eddies of these new settlements." Recent arrivals,
clustered by kin and county-of-origin. Presbyterians of every splinter, in
"destitute condition": they "had not, for a long time, had an opportunity of
partaking of the Lord's Supper." Not because there's no minister — because there's
no minister *of their exact sub-sect*. What they're missing is the same Scottish
*communion season* the class met at Cane Ridge: the multi-day sacramental occasion
that was the event of the religious year.

**2. The name.** Into this country comes a worn-out Irish minister — literally: the
strain of teaching, pastoring, and synod work gave him a debilitating illness, and
his physician prescribed a sea voyage as the only remedy. He sails from Londonderry
thirty-five days, leaving eighteen-year-old Alexander in charge of the family, and
lands at Philadelphia in May 1807, assigned to the frontier. His credentials: a
minister of the **Anti-Burgher Seceder Presbyterians**. Unpack the name slowly —
Presbyterian; but Seceder (split from the Church of Scotland); but Anti-Burgher
(split from the other Seceders over a civic oath required in a few Scottish towns).
Each hyphen is a communion table someone was barred from. Two ironies: he is already
a veteran of a failed unity campaign (in Ireland he worked for Burgher/Anti-Burgher
union; the Glasgow synod let him argue the case in person, then refused to let it
come to a vote) — and in America the quarrel in his sect's name was already
officially dead: the American synod had merged Burghers and Anti-Burghers, since the
oath didn't exist here. The fences of a buried quarrel are about to prosecute him
anyway.

### Movement II — The Table

**3. The invitation (August 1807, Cannamaugh).** A sacramental service ~70 miles
east of Pittsburgh. Campbell, moved by settlers of other Presbyterian branches long
deprived of the Supper, uses the preparation sermon to lament the divisions of the
church — and then invites "all his pious hearers, who felt so disposed and duly
prepared… without respect to party differences" to the table. The fence comes down
for an afternoon.

**4. The echo — and the difference.** This is Cane Ridge's Burke scene, six years
later, with the opposite ending. At Cane Ridge the gatekeepers pressed, Stone
withdrew the demand, and the table opened. Here, the young assisting minister,
William Wilson, says nothing publicly at the time — the complaint travels afterward,
by letter and gossip among the ministers. This time the fence fights back.

### Movement III — The Trial

**5. The machinery engages (October 1807).** Senior minister John Anderson refuses
even to keep a preaching appointment with Campbell, citing doctrines "inconsistent
with some articles of our testimony." The presbytery sides with Anderson and
appoints an investigating committee — Anderson plus three of his own former seminary
students. Campbell's preaching appointments are suspended, and with them his income.
He storms out of the meeting: not a marble saint — a temper and a wound.

**6. The Libel (January 1808).** A formal seven-count indictment. Among the charges:
he taught that assurance belongs to a high degree of faith, not its essence (you can
be saved without feeling certain of it); that "a church has no divine warrant for
holding Confessions of Faith as terms of communion" — the heart of the case; that
elders may lead vacant congregations; that laypeople may hear ministers of other
bodies. The detail that lands the system's absurdity: charge #1 prosecutes the
thesis of the prosecutor's own book — Anderson had written the 1793 treatise "The
Appropriation Which Is in the Nature of Saving Faith." The fight was about the whole
fence system, not one act of hospitality.

**7. His answers (February 1808).** Each a small theological essay. On faith, the
tight logical strike: if assurance were of faith's essence, "then none that had true
faith, could be possibly without it." On confessions, the sentence that is the
Declaration & Address in embryo: no command of Christ or the apostles "to make the
approbation of them a term of communion… as a sine qua non." The verdict: convicted
on the two counts that matter — assurance and creeds-as-fences; three more charges
he openly admitted and would not retract. His suspension was then made indefinite by
a rump session of three, reconstituted after Campbell had left and the meeting had
closed.

**8. The appeal (May 1808, Philadelphia).** The set-piece. Campbell stands before
the assembled Associate Synod and reads: *"Honored Brethren: Before you come to a
final issue… let me entreat you to pause a moment."* The week's idea in the
defendant's own voice — including his assurance that he asks nothing of them he
would impose in turn: *"Nor do I presume to dictate to them or to others as to how
they should proceed for the glorious purpose of promoting the unity and purity of
the Church"* — and the pointed reminder to a synod that had itself merged Burghers
and Anti-Burghers in America: *"I sincerely rejoice with them in what they have done
in that way; but still, all is not yet done; and surely they can have no just
objections to go farther."*

**9. The verdict — institutional havering.** The synod finds "such informalities in
the proceedings of the Presbytery… as to afford sufficient reason… to set aside
their judgment" — suspension lifted on procedure — then hands the papers to a
committee that finds his answers "evasive and unsatisfactory… sufficient grounds to
infer censure." Overturned *and* re-censured. He submits — under a signed
declaration that submission means "no more… than an act of deference to the judgment
of the court." Deference, not agreement.

**10. The grind (1808–1809).** He goes home; the presbytery still refuses him
appointments. Note-takers attend his meetings. September 1808: he presents a paper
denouncing presbytery and synod both. May 1809: he hands the synod a document
formally removing himself from its authority — abandoning "all ministerial
connection," holding himself "utterly unaffected by its decisions." He was never
expelled; after two years of the grind, the man who did not want to leave finally
walks.

### Movement IV — The Grove

**11. The maple grove.** And then — nothing stops. No pulpit, no salary, no
standing, and no interruption: *"Sometimes the deep shade of a maple grove sheltered
the assembly from the summer sun. Generally, however, the houses of his old Irish
neighbors… were the places where he had his appointments for preaching, and where he
discoursed weekly to all who chose to assemble."* The landing line: the church
courts took away everything they had power over — and it turned out to be
surprisingly little.

**12. Optional bookend.** On his first arrival at Washington, a woman introduces
herself: in Ireland she once walked six miles to attend a Seceder communion, and
remembers him serving it. The story starts and ends at a communion table.

### Movement V — Lessons

**13. The break began as hospitality, not doctrine.** He didn't set out to found
anything; he extended the Supper's benefits to believers the fence-system had
orphaned. When the institution's fences and the gospel's welcome conflict, the
fences are the thing without divine warrant.

**14. Non-creedalism is a *protective* move.** From the appeal itself: no human
formulation as a test of fellowship — because no one's interpretation is infallible,
and the simplest believer must be able to confess Christ and belong. Teach,
persuade, argue, disagree — everything except making your reading a barrier.

**15. A collision of goods.** The Seceders were not villains. Campbell had violated
rules he was ordained under; some of his answers were equivocal (the presbytery said
so, and reading them, you can see why); the system he broke was built, sincerely, to
protect the table's purity. Order vs. hospitality, both held by serious people — and
the man who opened the table genuinely did not want to leave. That's what makes it
cost something.

**16. Close — the tease.** That fall, the people who'd been following him from
farmhouse to grove asked him to write down what they stood for. The first sentence
of what he wrote: *"The Church of Christ upon earth is essentially, intentionally,
and constitutionally one."* We'll meet that document again.
