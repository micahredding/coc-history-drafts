## Entering the Practices (Weeks 9–12)

Everything so far has been the story of how the freedom was won. What remains is the harder question: what holds a church together once it has refused to be held together from above?

If we take the priesthood of all believers seriously — if we allow no mediators — then the priesthood has to be *practiced*, and practiced constantly. An office persists between meetings; a practice doesn't. So a church that dissolved its offices had to become a church that assembles often and does something every time.

And notice what got chosen. Someone can preach at you, decide for you, administer to you. But no one can be baptized for you, sing for you, read the text for you, or vote for you. The practices at the center of this movement are precisely the acts that admit no proxy. That is not an accident of history; it is the whole design.

What those acts produce is more than individual participation. When you sing, you hear that everyone around you has a voice. When you study, you know the person beside you can check the preacher's citation. Each practice performs your own priesthood and witnesses to everyone else's — and everyone knows that everyone knows. That mutual, public, repeated knowledge is the binding. It is what this movement has instead of a hierarchy, and the four weeks ahead are four different ways of doing it.

And the clearest case is the one practice that gets no week of its own, because it gets every week. In Week 1 we asked the table's first question — *who may come?* — and watched the test at the door get withdrawn. Here is its second question: *who may preside?* In the Catholic tradition that question is the definition of priesthood itself; the priest is the one who may stand at the altar, and the layman is the one who may not. Which means a church that hands the Supper to a farmer, a teenager, a man who fixes cars all week — and does it every Sunday, as a matter of course, without anyone thinking it remarkable — is not using "priesthood of all believers" as a slogan. It is the most literal enactment of that doctrine anywhere in Protestantism. Nearly everyone else said it and then kept an ordained man at the table for the sake of good order. We said it and meant it, fifty-two times a year, in front of everybody.

So the hospitality of the early weeks and the priesthood of these later ones are not two themes. They are one table, asked two questions, answered the same way both times: no one stands between you and it.

> **What a priest is — said carefully, by people who still believe it.** This is worth hearing in their own words, because it is not a strawman and it is not a small claim. The Catechism of the Catholic Church puts it in one sentence: *"Only validly ordained priests can preside at the Eucharist and consecrate the bread and the wine so that they become the Body and Blood of the Lord"* (§1411). And the Second Vatican Council is the fairest statement of the difference, because it grants almost everything we would want to say — there really is a "common priesthood of the faithful," it says, and then: the common priesthood and the ministerial priesthood *"differ from one another in essence and not only in degree,"* because "the ministerial priest, by the sacred power he enjoys, teaches and rules the priestly people; acting in the person of Christ, he makes present the Eucharistic sacrifice" (*Lumen Gentium* 10). Four centuries earlier the Council of Trent had drawn the line harder, defining priesthood by the altar rather than the pulpit: against any claim that the New Testament has no priesthood but "only an office and bare ministry of preaching the Gospel" (Session 23, Canon 1). So: Rome affirms the priesthood of all believers *and* reserves the altar. We affirm it and open the altar. That difference is the entire second half of this class.

And one more thing follows, and it is the honest ending rather than a triumphant one. If the priesthood belongs to every believer, and if it is conferred in baptism — which is where this very week begins — then *any* fence at the head of the table is precisely the sort of human addition this movement existed to remove. For most of our history "any member may preside" quietly meant *any man*. That this congregation now sets no such fence is not a modern borrowing grafted onto the tradition; it is the tradition's own founding logic finally cashed out — one more fence found and removed, by the same reasoning that removed all the others. Which should keep us from congratulating ourselves. The doctrine has always been more radical than the people holding it, and it is very likely still out ahead of us.

**The Story:** On the frontier, thousands sat in agony at the mourner's bench, waiting for a feeling that would prove they were saved. Walter Scott discovered an answer: a gospel you could respond to *today*. This was a liberation. God had provided a path of assurance — you can act, and in acting, know that God has forgiven you.

**The Idea**: Believer's Baptism embodies the Priesthood of All Believers right at the start. God empowers our own agency as the medium to express the assurance of salvation.

**Connections:**
- Back to Cane Ridge: the ecstatic experience of Week 1 has hardened into a boundary — the required experience as a new fence. Stone overcame the Presbyterian boundary by calling sinners simply to "come"; now that same overcoming is needed again, at the water.
- Believer's baptism elevates agency and consent — the priesthood of all believers at the moment of entry. God won't force you, and he also will not deny you.

**The Thread — the practice that *confers*:** Baptism is the one practice that happens only once, because it is the conferral itself: the moment the church's whole authority is placed in an ordinary person. No one can be baptized on your behalf; the agency has to be yours or the act is empty. And the congregation watches it happen — everyone present sees a new priest made, and knows that everyone else saw it too. Every practice that follows maintains the standing this one confers.

## Ideas

1. **Assurance, not performance.** Baptism is real because God acts in it — a pledge you can stand on, against every mourner's bench, then and now.
2. **Assurance is agency.** The first experience of conversion is your own choice — your agency validated in the deepest sense. God won't force you, and he will not deny you.
3. **The gate was opened before the gospel was preached through it.** The movement's first baptismal act removed a man-made barrier between a believer and the water.
4. **Hold both Campbells together.** No human rule between a believer and the water (1812) — and baptism itself must never become such a rule (1837): "not even immersion… a Pharisee among Christians."
5. **Most distinctive, most catholic.** "One baptism for the remission of sins" is the Nicene Creed's own confession — reaching for the original, we found ourselves holding the inheritance.
